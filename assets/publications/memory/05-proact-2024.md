# PROACT: adding physical-attack resistance to the cryptographic design flow

## Problem and motivation

IoT devices handle personal and business-critical data but are constrained in energy, latency, chip area, cost, and form factor. Countermeasures against physical attacks add overhead to each of those dimensions. Security can also extend the development schedule: traditional side-channel and fault evaluation often happens only after fabrication, so a detected weakness forces redesign and an expensive chip re-spin.

PROACT addresses a multi-objective design problem:

- maximize resistance to side-channel analysis and fault analysis;
- minimize energy, computational delay, and hardware resources;
- reduce time to market by identifying weaknesses before fabrication.

The chapter argues that these objectives must span the whole knowledge chain. Choices made in the cryptographic algorithm constrain circuit protections, physical implementation, and evaluation; feedback from measurements must in turn influence algorithms and hardware.

## Physical attacks in scope

The project distinguishes two main categories:

- **Side-channel analysis:** extracting secrets from physical observations such as power consumption, electromagnetic emanation, or execution timing.
- **Fault analysis:** deliberately perturbing computation with mechanisms such as laser injection, clock glitches, or supply glitches and using the resulting errors to reveal secrets or bypass protections.

Medical sensors, wearables, implants, and other physically accessible IoT devices are highlighted as motivating use cases.

## Proposed change to the design flow

Conventional hardware design automation optimizes area, timing, and energy but typically does not optimize physical security. PROACT adds physical-security feedback before tape-out. Its envisioned flow includes:

1. cryptographic algorithm selection/design;
2. protected circuit and software design;
3. implementation and normal power/performance/area optimization;
4. pre-silicon side-channel and fault simulation;
5. feedback and targeted redesign before fabrication;
6. post-silicon evaluation to validate the simulator and final security.

The intended outcome is a higher probability of first-time-right secure silicon rather than replacing final laboratory evaluation.

## Research questions

The paper frames six questions:

- How should algorithms be designed for resilience to physical attacks?
- Which circuits provide the best trade-off among physical security, energy, resources, and performance?
- How can design automation optimize circuit efficiency and physical security together?
- How can a pre-silicon security simulator balance accuracy and speed?
- How can AI improve that simulator’s accuracy and speed?
- Which advanced analysis methods should evaluate the chips and validate the simulator?

## Work package 1: algorithm design

WP1 collects requirements and feedback from circuit design, simulation, and evaluation and feeds them into cryptographic algorithm design. The chapter identifies several desired properties:

- low latency for applications such as memory/pointer encryption and for lower energy;
- low algebraic degree and structures suitable for masking or threshold implementations;
- efficient protection against sophisticated fault attacks such as SIFA, including constructions based on Toffoli gates plus masking;
- parallelism that enables shuffled operation order;
- equivalent internal representations that make repeated fault injection harder to control.

The point is not that one primitive simultaneously optimizes everything, but that physical implementation requirements should influence algorithm choices early.

## Work package 2: circuit design and the PROACT chip

WP2 develops a measurement and validation vehicle, called the PROACT chip. At the time of publication it was prototyped on FPGA and planned for ASIC fabrication.

The SoC architecture includes:

- a control RISC-V core (`Ctrl-RV`);
- a second Ibex RISC-V core (`SW-RV`) for cryptographic software;
- control and status registers;
- SPI for initialization and UART for host communication;
- instruction/data memories and cooperative measurement triggers;
- hardware cryptographic coprocessors, including AES, Ascon, and Xoodyak;
- protected and unprotected software/hardware benchmark candidates.

The control core loads programs/data, starts the target core or coprocessor, and waits for completion status. Carefully placed triggers support precise acquisition of power or other traces. The platform is intended to connect real post-silicon measurements to pre-silicon estimates at several abstraction levels.

## Work package 3: pre-silicon physical-security simulation

WP3 targets a simulator that accepts a design after synthesis, placement, and routing and predicts side-channel leakage and fault effects. AI is proposed to increase speed and accuracy for large designs, trained with measurements from fabricated benchmark circuits and their corresponding implementation data.

The project’s early state-of-the-art study found major gaps: no broadly available side-channel simulator for the project’s RISC-V architecture, heavy manual effort, difficulty modeling processor events such as stalls and mispredictions, and little evidence that AI had automated simulator construction.

The chapter reports related project outputs:

- **ABBY**, an open-source framework for collecting microarchitectural leakage-profiling data;
- ABBY-CM0 and ABBY-CM3 datasets covering instruction, operand, pipeline, and memory interactions on Arm Cortex-M0/M3 targets;
- evaluation of linear and nonlinear/deep-learning leakage models using statistical fit and actual attack outcomes;
- a comparison of four open-source fault simulators and recommendations inspired by AI-based cryptographic fault simulation.

These were intermediate foundations for the envisioned PROACT simulator, not a claim that the final simulator was already complete.

## Work package 4: evaluation and validation

WP4 studies how to evaluate physical security systematically, including deep-learning side-channel analysis. The chapter discusses:

- random/grid search, Bayesian optimization, reinforcement learning, and structured methodologies for neural-network hyperparameter tuning;
- the trade-off between trace feature engineering and model complexity;
- raw-trace versus selected-window training;
- attack-specific loss functions and metrics;
- profiled, non-profiled, model-free, collision, ensemble, and data-augmentation settings;
- interpretability of successful attacks as feedback for countermeasure design.

This work package supplies the attack/evaluation perspective needed to test both the PROACT chip and the pre-silicon simulator.

## Status reported in the paper

At publication time, the chapter reports:

- a system-on-chip design prototyped on FPGA and nearly ready for tape-out;
- analysis and comparison of existing physical-security simulators;
- improvements to physical-attack strategies;
- two planned tape-outs to validate the future physical-security simulator.

These status statements are time-specific to 2024.

- This is a project vision/status chapter, not a final validation of every planned tool or chip.
- At the stated 2024 snapshot, the SoC was on FPGA and nearly ready for tape-out; the two tape-outs and final simulator were planned future work. Do not rewrite planned outcomes as completed results.
- The chapter aggregates work from four work packages and many authors/institutions. It does not assign every result to an individual author.
- Pre-silicon evaluation is positioned as early feedback, not a replacement for post-silicon security evaluation.
